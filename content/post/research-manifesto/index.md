I've been meaning to write something about how grad school applications went for me for a little while, not because I'm in a position to review applications and tell anyone what to do but more because I learned some lessons that may be helpful for others. 

## About Me
I did my undergrad in ECE and an M.Eng in CS at Cornell; I wasn't an outstanding student for PhD applications (my GPA was like a 3.3 for undergrad, 3.9 for my M.Eng, which maybe helped offset it but also it's was a joint program with my BS that took one semester). I did undergrad research throughout my time at one lab, and also did several research-based projects as part of something called a project team. I wasn't sure I wanted to pursue a PhD after graduating at the time and instead was thinking about industry, so I opted for summer internships over summer research experiences after freshman year and though I understood publications would be good for my resume, I wouldn't say it was a goal of mine to present my research. I got more interested in neuroscience and neurotech in senior year, but my first job was in fintech before transitioning to biotech at Bionic Sight.  I worked there for 4 years before beginning my PhD.


# Before the Application
## What I Would've Done Differently in Undergrad
My career path was not a direct path to research, which I'm cool with because my industry experiences shaped my research goals. However, if I had a time machine I would probably have gone back and tweaked a few things:

1. Find ways to share what you're working on in undergrad! Paper authorship is of course amazing but posters, talks, conferences or even just chatting with people are really valuable too! I realized later on that research is a much more social endeavor than I had thought. Writing and creating figures are key figures even famous professors can miss on, scientific is communication a great skill to develop for anyone whether you end up pursuing a PhD or not. 

2. Breadth is great, but don't stretch yourself too thin. I tried exploring a lot of things and I learned a lot, but in retrospect I wish I had avoided some harder, "interesting" coursework or said no to some commitments so I could really cement my knowledge of the fundamentals at a steady pace. 

3. Don't compare yourself to other people. Unless you're the undergrad that's absolutely killing it, it can be demoralizing to look at other people's accomplishments and feel like you're incapable of contributing or that your scientific opinion is just wrong. 


## Choice of Department
One of the non-obvious things for me in applying to PhD programs was whether my research interests coincided with neuroscience, biomedical engineering, or electrical engineering. 


## Reaching out to PIs
Many people will give you this advice, but I think a lot of people do this in an unstructured way. There is essentially a depth-first (meeting people at conferences or reading interesting papers and contacting the authors) and breadth-first (go to a department website and search keywords) method to collect potential matches. Both have value, and you'll want to do both  thoroughly to evaluate potential fits maybe as far back as spring or summer before the application cycle. This is early, but for me, this was the most time-consuming thing about applying. It's nebulous at that point whether labs will be hiring grad students, so at this point you can make a big list and keep track of university/department and other relevant info. Next, there's value in using social media (Twitter and Reddit) to see who works on what - for example, a scientist you follow may retweet a colleague's job ad, or a direct Reddit post may give you ideas about faculty you hadn't considered. The reality of PhD programs and academics is even if you are really talented, a lab you like simply may not be able to hire you because they don't have an open project or funding. 

Around August, begin contact people with strong fits for you. For each email I spent, I'd spend a total of a few hours understanding their research goals, reading abstracts of their recent work, and for my top choices, I'd read a few papers fully. Nobody expects you to fully understand the work or memorize particulars, but you want to know enough that you can ask strong questions about next steps and demonstrate you can think critically. 

There are some great resources on what to actually say to people online, but you want to be authentic because faculty receive a lot of these emails. Since I was spending quite a bit of time writing these, I made my own "formula" which felt like a good balance: a one-sentence introduction of the program I applied to to and a few words on why I'd be a good fit, one or two sentences on which directions in the lab I find most compelling, and a concluding sentence, with my attached CV for reference. 

# The Application

## Knowing What You Want
I got confused as to how specific I should be in describing what I want: I had ideal experiments in mind that would be cool to do, or entire fields I would be excited to explore more. How specific should you be in describing your interests and future goals? For this, I'd recommend looking at other essays for similar programs, which may be available online. If not, there are sometimes applicant support groups with peer mentors, or you can even cold email grad students and see where it gets you (but make your intention clear, you shouldn't just copy them!) For your future goals, you want to show that a PhD is integral for your future career and interests. This is important because even if you don't get paid a lot, it's still an investment from a lab or department into your training, and it's something of a sunk cost on their end too if you end up quitting early. You should have an idea of a field of research you'd want to contribute to for 5-7 years way before you apply, but you can refine the details as you are writing. 

## Fellowships
If you're eligible, you should try to apply for at least one fellowship. Not only does this secure your own funding if you are successful, meaning you will have a lot more flexibility in schools and labs, it's also a great resume booster if you win and signals to departments you are a serious candidate. These applications are not a trivial amount of work, so you'll want to do it well in advance.

## Recommendations
Secure your recommendations way in advance of applying. A simple heads-up a few months before makes sure your busy recommender are willing to commit to the task. Also, if someone isn't willing to write you a rec, you'll leave yourself ample time to find a backup. It's probably ok to be up front asking people how often they want to be reminded of it, because it's easy to forget if you dont' mention it again for a while.
